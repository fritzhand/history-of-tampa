Ybor City

A company town that outlived its industry

In October 1885 a cigar manufacturer signed a contract with the booster club of a Florida town of about seven hundred people, bought forty acres of scrub north-east of it, and built a city there for one trade.

Era one
02 / 10
1885 – 1914

Boomtown

Vicente Martínez-Ybor contracted with the Tampa Board of Trade on 5 October 1885. Workmen felled trees and filled in alligator holes; Gavino Gutierrez laid out a grid of 200-by-350-foot blocks. Ignacio Haya’s factory reached production first, in March 1886. By the end of that year Ybor alone had built 176 worker houses and bought a streetcar line into Tampa. The town was annexed in June 1887 and kept its own languages anyway.

The work itself
03 / 10
1886 – 1931

The factory floor

Cigars were rolled by hand at long benches. At its peak the industry here employed twenty thousand people, making cigars in thirty-six sizes and shapes. On a platform at the front sat the lector — a reader the rollers paid out of their own wages to read newspapers and novels aloud across the floor all day.

The mutual aid societies
04 / 10
1891 – 1904

What they built for themselves

Six mutual aid societies were founded in thirteen years: El Centro Español in 1891, L’Unione Italiana in 1894, El Círculo Cubano in 1899, Los Libres Pensadores Martí-Maceo in 1900, Centro Asturiano in 1902 and La Unión Martí-Maceo in 1904. They sold prepaid medical care and built clubhouses, theatres and hospitals. The Centro Español sanatorium of 1906 was likely the most modern hospital in the region.

Era two
05 / 10
1915 – 1929

Metropolis

The great clubhouses went up in this period — Centro Asturiano with its 1,062-seat theatre in 1913, the Círculo Cubano in 1917, the Italian Club in 1918. It was also the decade the trade peaked, and the last one before the machines.

Era three
06 / 10
1930 – 1945

Depression

Cigarette consumption and cigar machinery undercut hand-rolling, and the Depression cut demand further. The 1931 strike ended the lector system. Through that decade a quarter of the foreign-born white population left the district.

Eras four and five
07 / 10
1946 – 1985

Postwar

The big factories left one by one — American Cigar in 1954 after fifty-three years in the Ybor complex, Hav-A-Tampa in 1972, General Cigar in 1976. Urban renewal cleared acreage in the 1960s and Interstate 4 was driven through. Some hands kept rolling regardless.

What was kept
08 / 10
1974 – 2026

Ybor City today

The district was listed on the National Register in 1974 and is now one of only three National Historic Landmark districts in Florida. It became a 133.1-acre redevelopment area in 1988, a streetcar line reconnected it to downtown in 2002, and a six-million-square-foot development is rising on its western edge.
